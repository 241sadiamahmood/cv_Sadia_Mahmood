PROJECT 01 · SAMPLE PROPOSAL
PROPOSED STUDENT LAB STUDY

Effect of Molasses Concentration on Yeast Fermentation

A low-cost industrial biotechnology study using sugarcane molasses and baker’s yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) to investigate fermentation activity as a model for bioethanol production.

Industrial BiotechnologyFermentationAgro-industrial By-product

Research question

How does the concentration of sugarcane molasses influence measurable fermentation activity by baker’s yeast under otherwise consistent laboratory conditions?

Why this study is justified

Sugarcane molasses is a sugar-rich by-product of the sugar industry. Investigating its use as a fermentation feedstock introduces students to microbial bioprocessing and the potential value addition of agro-industrial materials.

Objectives

  • Prepare a small range of molasses-based fermentation media.
  • Compare yeast fermentation activity across the selected concentrations.
  • Monitor pH, soluble solids (°Brix), and carbon dioxide production over time.
  • Practise recording, graphing, and interpreting experimental observations.

Suggested lab design

With supervisor approval, compare four labelled molasses concentrations (for example, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% w/v, defined consistently by the lab protocol) using the same yeast inoculum, working volume, temperature, and observation schedule. Include a no-yeast control. Use suitable fermentation vessels with an airlock or approved gas-release arrangement; never tightly seal ordinary bottles. Record observations at fixed intervals such as 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours.

Materials & techniques

Sugarcane molasses, commercial baker’s yeast, distilled water, balance, measuring cylinders, labelled fermentation flasks, pH meter or strips, thermometer, and (if available) a refractometer for °Brix. Compare results using tables and simple graphs.

How to interpret the data

Changes in °Brix, pH, and carbon dioxide production can indicate fermentation activity, but they do not directly prove or quantify ethanol. To report ethanol concentration or yield, use a validated ethanol assay or suitable analytical instrument available in the laboratory.

Expected learning outcomes

Students gain experience in media preparation, microbial handling, experimental controls, fermentation monitoring, data presentation, and the industrial relevance of converting an agro-industrial by-product into a potential biofuel feedstock. Actual outcomes must be determined from collected data.

Proposed student study only · No experiment has been claimed as completed and no results are fabricated.

PROJECT 02 · LAB STUDY
PROPOSED BASIC LAB STUDY

Evaluation of Amylase Activity on Starch Hydrolysis

A straightforward enzyme-biotechnology experiment assessing how pH or temperature affects starch breakdown using an amylase preparation and the iodine-starch test.

Enzyme BiotechnologyStarch HydrolysisColorimetric Observation

Research question

How does one selected factor, such as pH, influence the ability of an amylase preparation to hydrolyse starch under controlled laboratory conditions?

Why this study is justified

Amylases are industrially important enzymes used in food processing, brewing, textiles, detergents, and starch processing. A simple starch-hydrolysis assay helps students understand enzyme activity, experimental controls, and the effect of environmental conditions without requiring advanced instrumentation.

Objectives

  • Prepare a consistent starch substrate and amylase working solution.
  • Compare enzyme activity across a small set of supervisor-approved pH conditions (or temperatures, changing only one factor).
  • Use the iodine-starch colour reaction to observe remaining starch at fixed time points.
  • Record observations systematically and compare replicates using a table or graph.

Suggested laboratory design

Choose either pH or temperature as the independent variable. Keep starch concentration, enzyme volume, reaction time, and total reaction volume consistent. Include a substrate-only negative control and, where appropriate, an enzyme-inactivated control. Run replicates if materials permit. Add iodine reagent to small aliquots according to the institution’s approved protocol and compare colour intensity consistently against a simple reference scale or validated absorbance method if available.

Materials & techniques

Soluble starch, an amylase preparation approved for teaching use, buffer solutions, iodine-potassium iodide reagent, test tubes, pipettes, water bath or temperature-controlled area, timer, and PPE. Techniques include solution preparation, pipetting, controlled enzyme assays, visual endpoint comparison, and data recording.

Data analysis & limitations

Record the colour response at each condition and compare replicate observations. A fading blue-black iodine-starch colour suggests less intact starch, but visual scoring is semi-quantitative. Do not describe it as an exact enzyme activity value unless a validated quantitative assay and calibration are used.

Expected learning outcomes

Students practise enzyme assay design, controls, repeatability, careful pipetting, and interpretation of how process conditions affect an industrially relevant enzyme. The actual optimum, if any, must be determined from experimental data.

Proposed teaching-laboratory study · Complete only with supervisor approval and approved reagent-handling procedures.

PROJECT 03 · RESEARCH PROPOSAL
PROPOSED RESEARCH CONCEPT

Development of Starch-Based Biodegradable Films

A low-cost biomaterials proposal exploring how glycerol concentration influences the physical properties of starch-based films made from an accessible starch source.

BiomaterialsBiodegradable MaterialsProduct Development

Research question

How does changing the amount of glycerol plasticizer affect the flexibility, thickness, and water response of a starch-based film prepared using a consistent formulation process?

Why this proposal is justified

Conventional plastic persistence creates interest in renewable, biodegradable material alternatives. Starch is widely available and can form films, while glycerol is commonly used as a plasticizer. This small-scale study offers a practical introduction to biopolymer formulation and material characterization using basic equipment.

Objectives

  • Prepare starch-based films using a consistent heating and casting method.
  • Compare a small number of glycerol concentrations while keeping other ingredients and processing conditions constant.
  • Measure film thickness and assess flexibility using a defined simple method.
  • Compare water absorption or water sensitivity using equal-sized samples and a consistent exposure time.
  • Identify formulation trade-offs from the recorded observations rather than assuming one formulation is best.

Proposed approach

Prepare a starch slurry with water and a small, supervisor-approved range of glycerol levels. Heat and mix using the department’s approved teaching protocol until the mixture becomes uniform, cast equal volumes onto level labelled trays, and dry under consistent conditions. Condition the films before testing. Use at least three independently prepared samples per formulation where feasible.

Materials & techniques

Food-grade starch (such as corn or potato starch), glycerol, distilled water, beakers, balance, hot plate, stirring rod, level casting plates or trays, ruler or micrometer, and PPE. Techniques include formulation, controlled heating, film casting, drying, thickness measurement, and simple physical-property testing.

Evaluation & limitations

Compare thickness, flexibility, and water response across formulations. These tests characterize preliminary film properties only. Do not claim a material is biodegradable based solely on its starch content or appearance; biodegradability requires a suitable, documented degradation or composting test under defined conditions.

Expected research value

The proposal links renewable raw materials with biomaterials development and helps students practise formulation design, controlled comparisons, measurement, and scientific reporting. Results and conclusions should be added only after the study is conducted.

Research proposal only · No film performance data or biodegradation results are claimed.
